# "The Nickel Trap" (LTCM 1998): script v1 draft (2026-11-15)

Niche: educational quant finance, cartoon-3d-short format, about 60 s, vertical, English, directed expressive narrator.
Not investment advice: the short teaches an idea (leverage and bet size), it never tells anyone what to buy.
Sequel link: callback to "The Shoe Computer" (Thorp sized his bets so one bad night could not wipe him out).

## Facts and sources
- **Fund.** Long-Term Capital Management (LTCM), a hedge fund in Greenwich, Connecticut. Founded by John Meriwether, began
  trading 24 Feb 1994 with just over $1 billion. Board members Myron Scholes and Robert Merton shared the 1997 Nobel
  Prize in Economics for the Black-Scholes option pricing model.
  - [Wikipedia: Long-Term Capital Management](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Long-Term_Capital_Management)
- **Returns.** About 21%, 43% and 41% a year after fees in its first three years (1995 and 1996: 43% and 41%).
- **Strategy.** Convergence trades: buy the cheaper of two almost identical bonds, short the dearer, bet the gap closes.
  Compared to "picking up nickels in front of a bulldozer" (Lowenstein, When Genius Failed, 2000).
- **Leverage.** Start of 1998: about $4.7bn equity, about $129bn assets, over $124bn borrowed (more than 25 to 1).
  Derivatives notional about $1.25 trillion.
- **Trigger.** Russia defaulted on its debt on 17 August 1998. Investors fled to safe bonds, so the gaps widened.
- **Loss.** About $1.85bn lost by end of August. About $4.6bn lost in less than four months, equity down to about $0.4-0.6bn.
- **Rescue.** 23 Sep 1998: 14 financial institutions put in about $3.65bn, organised by the New York Fed. It was a private
  rescue, not taxpayer money. The fund was liquidated in early 2000.
- Verify note: sources differ slightly (3.6 vs 3.65 bn; 1997 return 17 vs 27%). Script says "about" and avoids 1997.

## Catchy checklist
- Hook line 1: a paradox (geniuses + Nobel Prizes + lost billions in four months).
- Nickels metaphor planted in line 4, paid off in the last line (loop).
- Callback to the shoe short in line 13.
- Gags all through: a vacuum for nickels, a tower of coins, a steamroller.

## Script
In 1998, a fund packed with geniuses lost four point six billion dollars in four months. Two of them had Nobel Prizes.
It was called Long-Term Capital Management. Professors, a famous bond trader, a Nobel-winning formula.
Their trick: find two almost identical bonds with a tiny price gap, and bet the gap closes.
The gaps were tiny. Like picking up nickels. So they borrowed. A lot.
For every dollar they owned, they borrowed twenty-five.
It worked. Over forty percent a year. Banks begged to lend them more.
Then, in August 1998, Russia defaulted on its debt.
The maths said that was nearly impossible. So everyone ran to safe bonds, and the gaps got wider instead of closing.
By the end of August, they had lost almost two billion dollars.
Borrowing twenty-five times means every loss hits twenty-five times harder.
Four months later, almost everything was gone.
The Federal Reserve called the big banks. Fourteen of them paid about three point six five billion dollars, to stop a crash.
Remember Ed Thorp? Same maths, but he sized every bet so one bad night couldn't wipe him out.
Being right is not enough. How big you bet decides if you survive.
Picking up nickels is fine. Just check where the steamroller is.
