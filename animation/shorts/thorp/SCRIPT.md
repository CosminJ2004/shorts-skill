# "The Shoe Computer": script v1 draft (2026-11-14)

Niche: educational quant finance, cartoon-3d-short format, about 55 s, vertical, English, directed expressive narrator.
Not investment advice: the short teaches an idea (edge, bet sizing), it never tells anyone what to buy.

## Facts and sources
- **Who.** Edward O. Thorp (maths professor) and Claude Shannon (founder of information theory) built a roulette
  predictor in 1960-61.
  - [Wikipedia: Edward O. Thorp](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edward_O._Thorp)
- **The device.** 12 transistors in a small plastic box carried in a pocket. The timing switches were hidden in the
  shoes and worked with the big toes. It timed the spinning ball to predict where it would land.
  - [MIT Museum](https://mitmuseum.mit.edu/collections/object/2007.030.014)
- **The edge.** Thorp's own account: an expected gain of +44% when betting on the most favoured octant.
- **Timeline.** Final version tested in Shannon's basement lab, June 1961. Las Vegas test, August 1961. The device
  worked, but they were so nervous about being caught that they dropped the project. Thorp made it public in 1966.
- **Record.** Guinness World Records recognises it as the first wearable computer. The original is in the MIT Museum.
  - [Guinness](https://guinnessworldrecords.com/world-records/564299-first-wearable-computer)
- **Thorp's later work.** Option pricing, the Kelly criterion, statistical arbitrage, inefficient markets (Wikipedia).
- **Today.** Prediction-market volume has exploded in 2026 and quant firms (Susquehanna, DRW, Flow Traders named by
  trade press) are building desks for event contracts.
  - [Finance Magnates](https://www.financemagnates.com/fintech/wall-street-quants-move-into-prediction-markets-to-hunt-for-arbitrage-not-to-bet)
  - [AI-CIO](https://www.ai-cio.com/?p=102989)

## Verified (2026-11-14)
- American double-zero roulette has a 5.26% house edge on most bets (several sources; European 2.70%). Line 7 says "about five".
- Kelly criterion: a rule to size bets to maximise long-run growth, f = (bp - q)/b, only valid when the probabilities
  are known ([Wikipedia: Kelly criterion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kelly_criterion)). Line 13 is the plain-words version.
- Prediction markets: keep it generic ("trading firms"), no firm names in the script.

## Catchy checklist
- Hook in line 1 (a concrete odd image: computer in a shoe) plus the payoff promise.
- Gags all through: toes clicking, nervous quitting, the museum smartwatch, final shoe check.
- Loop: last line returns to the shoes.

## Script
In 1961, two geniuses hid a computer in their shoes. To beat roulette.
One of them, Claude Shannon, had just invented information theory. Yes, the maths behind your phone.
The other, Ed Thorp, was a maths professor who thought casinos were just physics problems.
The idea: roulette isn't pure luck. Time the ball and the wheel, and you can guess which slice it lands in.
Their computer had twelve transistors. The switches were in their shoes. They clicked them with their big toes.
In the basement test, it gave them a forty-four percent edge.
Forty-four percent. A normal player loses about five.
So in August, they flew to Las Vegas.
It worked. And then they got so nervous about being caught... that they quit.
Thorp kept the secret for five years. Guinness now calls it the first wearable computer.
It's in a museum at MIT, a great-grandfather of your smartwatch.
But here's the real lesson. Thorp stopped betting on wheels, and started betting on markets.
Same trick: find a small edge, then size every bet so one bad night can't wipe you out.
Today, trading firms run that same playbook on prediction markets.
So next time someone says it's all luck... check their shoes.
